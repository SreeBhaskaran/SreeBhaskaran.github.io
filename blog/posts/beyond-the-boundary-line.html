Beyond the Boundary Line

Lessons from cricket, analytics, and decision-making under uncertainty.

May 7, 2026 Analytics Operations Decision-Making Supply Chain

Sports and Business Aren’t That Different

I am a huge sports fan; I love to play and even more to watch. The tension, the uncertainty, and the exhilaration that come with watching or playing are unlike almost anything else. But beyond the beauty of sports and the thrill it offers, another thing I enjoy most is that sports often mirror business problems more closely than we realize.

As distinct as these worlds may seem, there is perhaps far more in common between them than we recognize, and more importantly, there is a great deal each can learn from the other. Questions around strategy, incentives, risk-taking, execution under pressure, and performance evaluation all show up in sports in a very visible way. Perhaps more importantly, analytics is becoming central to modern sports as teams search for every marginal edge possible — something businesses have also been doing for decades in operations, supply chains, and decision-making.

The IPL: A Laboratory for Analytics

Cricket is the most popular sport in India, and over the last two decades, one format in particular has transformed the game: Twenty20 (T20) cricket, where each team faces only 20 overs, or 120 deliveries. The shortened format dramatically changes the economics and strategy of the sport. Every ball matters more, momentum shifts faster, and decisions become far more consequential.

At the center of this transformation is the Indian Premier League (IPL), which has grown into one of the most valuable sports leagues in the world. Houlihan Lokey’s 2025 IPL Brand Valuation Study estimated the IPL’s business enterprise value at $18.5 billion, up 12.9% year over year, underscoring its commercial scale and global reach.

The IPL has also become a fascinating laboratory for analytics-driven decision-making. Because the format is so compressed, even small strategic advantages can meaningfully alter outcomes. Teams increasingly rely on data to optimize player matchups, batting order decisions, bowling changes, field placements, and risk-taking strategies.

In many ways, this mirrors how businesses use analytics in operations management and supply chains: when margins are tight and uncertainty is high, even small improvements in decision-making can create substantial competitive advantages.

When every ball matters, analytics becomes less about description and more about decision-making.

Are Sixes in the Death Overs Really Worth Less?

Against this backdrop, I recently came across an interesting ESPNcricinfo article titled “How much is a six worth in the IPL? Not always six runs.” The article argues that the value of a six changes depending on the context of the game. A six early in an innings may significantly alter projected scores, while a six in the death overs may matter less because higher scoring rates are already “priced in” at that stage of the innings.

In many ways, I found it to be a fascinating application of analytics and contextual valuation. Incorporating context into decisions and actions is extremely important, particularly in environments characterized by uncertainty and unpredictable outcomes. This is true not only in sports, but also in operations management, innovation, and supply chain decision-making. Rarely does the same action carry identical value across all situations.

Yet, despite appreciating the analytical framing of the article, I found myself disagreeing with one important part of its premise. Not with the idea that context matters — it absolutely does. In fact, within the field of operations management, context is everything. The value of inventory during a disruption is different from its value during stable demand. The value of excess manufacturing capacity changes depending on congestion, uncertainty, and demand variability. Similarly, as the article argues, a six at 230/2 is obviously different from a six at 90/5.

Where I disagree is with the argument that sixes are somehow worth less in the death overs because scoring rates are already expected to be high. If anything, I would argue the opposite.

The article’s reasoning is rooted in how projections change over the course of an innings. Early in an innings, a six can materially alter the expected final score because it helps create a stronger platform from which the team can build. There is still significant uncertainty about how the innings will unfold, and starting from a higher base creates greater scoring potential.

By contrast, in the death overs, when teams are already expected to score rapidly, an additional six may not dramatically change projected totals because aggressive scoring is already “built into” the expectation models. In other words, the argument is that teams are already expected to score at a high rate, so an additional six carries less incremental value relative to expectations.

The Cost of Failure Matters More

However, I believe this framing misses some important points.

To begin with, the correct comparison should not be between a six and an already elevated scoring expectation. The real comparison should be between a six and a possible alternative outcome — often a dot ball or even a wicket. In the death overs, failing to score quickly can be disastrous. Teams have only a limited number of deliveries left to make an impact, and a missed opportunity is gone forever. Pressure compounds immediately, shot selection changes, bowlers gain leverage, and the consequences cascade through the remaining deliveries. In that sense, the value of a six may actually be amplified in the later overs precisely because the cost of failure becomes much higher. In short, while the difficulty of hitting the shot may remain the same, the consequence of failure becomes far greater. In fact, the article itself indirectly points toward this idea when it notes that “a dot ball at the death would actually hurt the trajectory more than a six helps it.” In many ways, that observation reinforces the importance of downside risk in high-pressure environments.

This distinction reflects a broader principle that also appears frequently in operations and supply chain management: organizations should care just as much about downside risk and failure prevention as they do about improving expected averages. An evaluation based purely on projections or expected-value improvements may undervalue actions whose primary role is to reduce vulnerability rather than simply increase average performance.

Supply Chains, Innovation, and Optionality

The process behind building a resilient supply chain provides a useful example. During normal periods, maintaining safety stock may appear inefficient when evaluated purely through an expected-value lens. Excess inventory increases holding costs and may seem unnecessary when disruptions are infrequent.

Yet during periods of uncertainty or disruption, that same inventory suddenly becomes enormously valuable because it prevents stockouts, production shutdowns, and cascading operational failures. Its value lies not merely in improving expected performance, but also in protecting the system from catastrophic downside risk.

The same logic applies to innovation and experimentation. Companies often undervalue exploratory projects because most experiments fail when viewed individually. From a purely average-outcome perspective, many innovation investments may appear inefficient. But what this ignores is the inherently option-like nature of such initiatives. Even a small number of successful ideas may generate disproportionate long-term impact.

Firms that focus only on average projected returns risk underinvesting in experimentation and, over time, sacrificing adaptability and long-term competitiveness. In such cases, the value of experimentation lies not only in immediate expected gains, but also in preserving upside potential and future strategic flexibility.

A projection is not independent of the action — it is partially created by it.

The Bigger Problem: Evaluating Decisions vs Outcomes

The other issue I have is more subtle. The core argument of the article is premised on evaluating actions primarily through projected outcomes. But projections are still just that — projections.

Using expected values or projected outcomes as a basis for evaluation is extremely useful in situations where outcomes are largely driven by factors outside an individual’s control. In such environments, evaluating decisions based on expectations rather than realized outcomes often makes sense because expectations better reflect the quality of decision-making under uncertainty.

However, I am not entirely convinced that the same logic applies cleanly in this setting. In cricket, particularly in the death overs, the projections themselves are shaped dynamically by the strategic choices players make ball after ball. The expected score is not an external condition imposed on the players; rather, it evolves based on the very decisions being evaluated.

The truly fixed constraints are limited — the number of deliveries remaining, pitch conditions, swing, weather, and other environmental factors. Everything else — scoring rates, pressure, momentum, field placements, and risk-taking — is endogenous to the decisions players make during the innings. In other words, the projection is not independent of the action — it is partially created by it.

The Same Mistake Happens in Organizations

Ironically, businesses often make a very similar mistake when evaluating people and organizational decision-making. We evaluate managers and employees almost entirely on realized outcomes, rather than their decision makign process, even when many of those outcomes were outside their control. A supply chain manager may make all the right decisions based on the information available at the time and still suffer disruptions because of geopolitical shocks, weather events, or supplier failures. Similarly, an innovation team may pursue a well-reasoned experiment grounded in strong assumptions and disciplined analysis, only for the market to evolve differently than expected. Yet organizations frequently judge these situations almost entirely through the lens of outcomes.

Good operations management teaches us that decisions should be evaluated based on the information available at the time, the logic underlying the decision, and the quality of the decision-making process itself, rather than through the lens of hindsight. Even a great decision may still result in a poor outcome, not because the decision itself was flawed, but because uncertainty is unavoidable. Conversely, a poor decision can occasionally produce a good outcome simply because of luck. Yet organizations consistently fall into the trap of “Monday morning quarterbacking.”

Beyond Cricket

The deeper lesson here is not really about cricket or sixes. It is about how we think about value under uncertainty. Whether in sports, supply chains, innovation, or management, focusing only on projections can obscure what else could be important: downside risk, optionality, resilience, and the quality of the decision-making process itself. That, perhaps, is the most valuable lesson sports analytics can teach business.
